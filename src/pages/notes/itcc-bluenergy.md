## The setup

TradingCo, a global trading house, had just acquired BluEnergy, a fuel distributor at Port Maren in the case market of Saraya. The investment committee had authorised $90m and two projects were on the table:

- **Option A:** a dedicated jetty that can take LR1 tankers, for $85m.
- **Option B:** 250,000 barrels of extra storage, for $67m.

The previous management team had backed storage. We went the other way.

## Three constraints, and which option touches them

Every problem in the case pack traced back to three constraints:

| Constraint | What it costs today | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Berth:** one jetty shared with a container terminal, first come first served | $2.1m a year of congestion demurrage | Solved: our own berth, our own schedule | Untouched |
| **Origin:** 100% of supply from UGE, on smaller MR tankers, as a sub-5% lifter | $2.00/bbl freight plus a $0.40/bbl small-lifter premium | Solved: LR1 freight and access to other origins | Untouched |
| **Tankage:** 500,000 bbl, diesel above 80% utilisation | $1.4m a year of ullage demurrage | Solved by converting one jet tank | Solved for $67m |

Option B buys a fix for the third constraint and leaves the two that define the business exactly as they are.

## The demurrage bill, taken apart

BluEnergy pays $3.5m a year in demurrage. It isn't one problem, it's two:

- **$2.1m is congestion.** Our tankers queue behind roughly 180 container calls a year. Owning the berth removes the queue.
- **$1.4m is ullage.** We buy cargoes free on board at the refinery, so the barrels are ours at sea and the ship waits until our tanks have room. If TradingCo buys at the refinery and we take delivery ex-ship, we only lift what we can store and the ship sails on.

The tank farm turned out to be misallocated rather than too small: jet fuel is 19% of throughput but holds 36% of capacity. Converting one 60,000 bbl jet tank to diesel costs an estimated $60–160k and doubles diesel headroom. That fixes a $1.4m-a-year problem for a rounding error next to Option B’s $67m.

## Freight and supply risk

LR1 access halves freight from $2.00/bbl to $1.00/bbl. On BluEnergy’s 7.8m bbl a year book, that’s $15.6m a year falling to $7.8m at full LR1. We assumed only 30% LR1 utilisation in the base case, a $2.34m annual saving.

The bigger argument was resilience. If UGE supply stopped in a Gulf disruption, Option A could replace barrels on LR1s from alternative origins for about $15.1m a year in freight, slightly *less* than today. Option B would be stuck on MR tankers into a congested berth, at about $26.5m.

## What Option B gets right

Option B isn’t a bad project. It delivers about $5.3m a year, mostly from strategic stock payments, and needs no negotiation with anyone. Its real cost is closer to $90.8m once you fill the new tanks.

If BluEnergy were still independent, we would have recommended it. Three of Option A’s four value drivers (LR1 freight, ex-ship buying and new supply origins) only exist because TradingCo owns the business.

## The desk framing

That ownership is the whole point. A dedicated LR1 jetty turns Port Maren into a node in TradingCo’s network: it can move diesel to whichever terminal pays the highest netback, run freight arbitrage, and route crude to the refinery that values it most. In the deck we priced one example: re-routing diesel to a short TradingCo terminal for a net spread of $1.85/bbl, about $0.56m on a 300,000 bbl parcel.

Storage is a fixed return. The jetty is an option.

> Option B is an insurance premium. Option A is an option. We do not recommend buying insurance.
